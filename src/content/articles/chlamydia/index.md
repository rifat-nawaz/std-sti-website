## What is chlamydia?

Chlamydia is an infection caused by the bacterium *Chlamydia trachomatis*. It spreads through vaginal, anal and oral sex, and from a pregnant person to their baby during birth.

It can infect the cervix, urethra, rectum, throat and, rarely, the eyes.

It's the most commonly reported bacterial STD in the US. The CDC counted 1,648,568 cases in 2023, and 55.8% of them were in people aged 15 to 24.

## Symptoms

**Most people have no symptoms at all.** That's why chlamydia spreads so easily and why routine testing matters.

When symptoms do show up, they usually start 1 to 3 weeks after sex:

| Where | Possible symptoms |
|---|---|
| Vagina or cervix | Unusual discharge, burning when peeing, bleeding between periods or after sex, pain during sex |
| Penis | Discharge, burning when peeing, pain or swelling in one testicle (less common) |
| Rectum | Pain, discharge or bleeding |
| Throat | Usually none; sometimes a sore throat |

Symptoms can be mild and come and go. Feeling better doesn't mean the infection has cleared.

## Why treatment matters

Untreated chlamydia can spread from the cervix to the uterus and fallopian tubes. This is called pelvic inflammatory disease (PID). PID can cause:

- Long-term pelvic pain
- Ectopic pregnancy, which can be life-threatening
- Infertility

In people with a penis, chlamydia can occasionally cause epididymitis, a painful swelling next to the testicle.

Chlamydia also raises the chance of getting or passing on HIV. In pregnancy, it can be passed to the baby at birth and cause eye and lung infections.

## Who should get tested

The US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) and the CDC recommend:

- **Sexually active women 24 and under:** every year.
- **Women 25 and older:** every year if at higher risk, for example with a new partner, more than one partner, or a partner who has an STD.
- **Pregnant people:** at the first prenatal visit if 24 or under, or at higher risk.
- **Men who have sex with men:** at least once a year at every site used for sex (urine, rectum, throat), and every 3 to 6 months if at higher risk.
- **Anyone with symptoms, or whose partner has chlamydia:** now.

## How testing works

The standard test is a **NAAT** (nucleic acid amplification test). It finds the bacteria's genetic material and is very accurate.

- **Vaginal swab:** the preferred sample for people with a vagina. You can collect it yourself.
- **Urine:** the usual sample for people with a penis. Don't pee for at least an hour beforehand.
- **Rectal and throat swabs:** needed if you've had anal or oral sex. A urine test won't find an infection in those places.

Testing too soon can give a false negative. Most guidance suggests testing about **2 weeks after a possible exposure**. Use our [window period calculator](/std-testing/window-period/) to check your dates.

You can test at a clinic, through a health department, or with a lab-processed home kit.

## Getting treated

Chlamydia is **curable**. A clinician, such as your doctor, a sexual health clinic or a telehealth service, prescribes the treatment and explains how to take it.

After a diagnosis:

1. **Don't self-treat.** Leftover or online antibiotics can be the wrong medicine, and a failed treatment lets the infection keep spreading.
2. **Follow your clinician's instructions** and finish everything they prescribe, even if you feel better.
3. **Avoid sex** until you and your partners have finished treatment and your clinician says it's safe.
4. **Tell recent partners** so they can be tested and treated. See [how to tell a partner](/guides/telling-a-partner/).
5. **Get retested about 3 months later.** Reinfection is common, often from a partner who wasn't treated.

Many states let clinicians provide treatment for your partner too, without a separate visit. Ask about it when you're diagnosed.

## Prevention

- Use condoms or dental dams every time you have sex.
- Get tested regularly, and ask new partners to test too.
- Make sure partners are treated before you have sex again.
- If you're a man who has sex with men or a transgender woman and had a bacterial STD in the last year, ask a clinician what prevention options fit you, including options taken after sex.
