## Three kinds of home test

| Type | How it works | Accuracy |
|---|---|---|
| Mail-in lab kit | You collect urine, swabs or a finger-prick blood sample and mail it to a lab | Same lab tests as clinics, if the sample is good |
| Order online, test at a lab | You order online and visit a local lab for a blood draw and urine sample | Same as a clinic |
| Rapid self-test | You do the whole test at home and read the result in minutes | Good for some uses, but most need a lab test to confirm a positive |

## What affects accuracy

1. **Timing.** Testing before the [window period](/std-testing/window-period/) ends is the most common cause of a false negative.
2. **Sample collection.** Follow instructions exactly. For urine, don't pee for at least an hour beforehand. For finger-prick blood, fill the tube to the line.
3. **Body sites.** If you've had oral or anal sex, you need throat and rectal swabs. Urine won't find those infections.
4. **Shipping.** Mail samples the same day, and avoid weekends if the kit says so.

## How to tell if a kit is legit

- **Lab certification:** the lab should be CLIA-certified. CAP accreditation is a plus.
- **FDA status:** rapid tests you read yourself should be FDA-authorized.
- **Clinician support:** a doctor or nurse should be available to explain results and prescribe treatment.
- **Clear test list:** it should say exactly which infections and which samples are included.
- **Privacy:** check how results are stored, and whether data is shared for marketing.

Be wary of cheap rapid tests sold online without FDA authorization. Their accuracy is often unknown.

## When a clinic is the better choice

- You have symptoms, especially sores, pain or fever
- You're pregnant
- A partner has tested positive and you need treatment now
- You had a possible HIV exposure in the last 72 hours
- Cost matters more than convenience. See [free testing options](/std-testing/free-testing/)
